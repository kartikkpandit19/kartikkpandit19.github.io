The most underrated shift in software right now isn't the AI itself.

It's where the bottleneck moved.

For thirty years, the binding constraint on building enterprise software was simple: how fast can humans type code. Companies needed armies of engineers because the work was largely typing translating clear requirements into working systems, plumbing databases, wiring authentication, handling edge cases. The thinking was 10%; the typing was 90%.
AI coding assistants flipped the ratio.

The typing is essentially free now. The constraint is no longer how fast you can produce code, it's how clearly you can think about what to build, who you're building it for, and what trade-offs matter.
Which is interesting, because most engineering hiring is still optimized for the old bottleneck.
We test people on syntax. On algorithm puzzles. On their ability to produce code under time pressure...exactly the part that's becoming a commodity.
Meanwhile the actual scarce skill...product taste, system design judgment, knowing when a feature is wrong before a single line is written is harder than ever to find, and we barely interview for it.
The teams who win the next decade won't be the ones with the most engineers. They'll be the ones who figured out which 10% to hire for.
